About this guide

Most of the time lost with Claude goes on repeating context and correcting the same things. An hour of set-up removes most of it.

This guide shows how to give Claude a lasting picture of your research, rules it applies every time, and a writing and visual style that is recognisably yours. It uses the Claude app on the web or the desktop app. Product details change quickly, so everything here was checked on 12 October 2026 and the sources let you re-check it.

Where your rules live

Claude reads your preferences from several places, each with a different reach. Choosing the right place for each rule is what makes Claude consistent.

Instructions for Claude
Under Settings, applied to every conversation. Keep it short and stable: who you are, your language and units, how you like answers.
Skills
Instructions, with files such as templates or style sheets, that Claude loads when a request matches them. They suit anything detailed that you need often but not in every chat, such as your writing and figure styles.
Projects
Instructions and files for one paper, grant or project. Each project also keeps its own memory, separate from your other chats.

A starting point for Instructions for Claude, which you can adapt:

Instructions for Claude
I am a researcher in [field], working on [one-line description of your
research]. Use British English and SI units. Be direct and concise, and tell
me when you are unsure or when the evidence is weak. When I ask for ideas,
give several options with pros and cons before recommending one. Never
invent references, data or results.

Teach Claude about your research

A research profile is a document of a page or two that tells Claude who you are as a researcher. Claude can write most of it by interviewing you.

The one-line description in your instructions follows you into every chat, but there is no room there for detail. The full profile goes into the knowledge of each project where your background matters, such as a paper, a proposal or a new collaboration. Because each project keeps its own memory, this written profile is the reliable way for Claude to know you everywhere.

Let Claude interview you

Gather your CV, your publication list (ideally with abstracts), a few past proposals or reports, and any web pages about your work. Start a chat, attach them and paste this:

The profile template27 lines
Research profile template
# Research profile: [your name]
Last updated: [date]

## Summary
Two or three sentences on who I am as a researcher and what I am known for.

## Areas of expertise
- Area: methods I use, questions I work on, key papers

## Past projects
- Title, funder and scheme, dates, my role, main outcomes

## Current work
- Project, stage, partners, what is still unpublished (in general terms)

## Collaborators
- Name, organisation, expertise, what we have done together

## Track record highlights
- Papers, software, datasets, patents, prizes, impact on practice or policy

## Resources
- Facilities, equipment, datasets, computing I can use

## Direction
- Where I want my research to go in the next five years
- Topics I have decided not to pursue, and why

Keep it accurate

Read every line, correct anything marked [check] and anything Claude has overstated, because Claude will repeat this profile about you. Save it as research-profile.md, or as a Google Doc, which stays in sync when added to a private project from Drive. Update it when a paper is accepted or a grant starts by asking: "Here is my current profile and a new paper. Update the profile and show me only what changed." The Direction section matters most, since it stops Claude steering you back to your past work.

Memory complements the profile. Turn it on under Settings > Memory, tell Claude "remember that…" for anything specific, and check what it has stored under Topics on the same page. Treat memory as a helper and the profile as the record you have checked.

Make Claude follow your rules

Claude follows rules that are specific, explained and few. Vague or conflicting rules are where consistency breaks down.

Weak

"Write well and be concise."

Strong

"Keep abstracts between 150 and 250 words. Open with the problem, give the main result with its numbers, and end with what it changes for the field."

A good rule is one you could check. Say why it matters ("reviewers in my field expect effect sizes"), describe what you want rather than what to avoid, and show a short example when the rule is about style. Keep the list short: ten rules that always hold work better than forty that compete. When two places disagree, such as your account instructions and a project's, Claude has to guess, so remove the conflict.

Test, check and correct

After writing a rule, try it on a real task and read the result against it. Rules weaken in very long chats, so start a new chat for each task. When a reply misses, ask Claude to check itself:

If you find yourself making the same correction twice, it belongs in a rule. Ask Claude to word it ("turn this correction into a rule for my instructions") and paste it into the right place. Every few months, read your rules again and delete the ones you no longer need.

A consistent writing style

Claude's default prose is fluent and generic. A written style guide, packaged as a skill, makes it sound like you in every chat.

Let Claude describe how you write

Gather four or five pieces of your own writing in different formats, such as a paper introduction, a lay summary, a proposal summary and a few emails, and ask:

Correct the draft, then add the rules your field or group expects: spelling, preferred terms, citation style and the formats you write most.

Package it as a skill

Ask Claude to turn the style guide into a skill called my-writing-style, and include two or three paragraphs of your writing as examples. Save it and switch it on under Customize > Skills, after turning on Code execution and file creation under Settings > Capabilities. Claude then loads it whenever you ask for text. Naming it in a request ("use my writing style") makes sure. The core of the skill looks like this:

The core of the writing-style skillSKILL.md, 26 lines
SKILL.md
---
name: my-writing-style
description: Use whenever I ask you to write or edit text for my research,
  including papers, proposals, abstracts, emails, slides and lay summaries.
---

## Voice
- "We" in papers, "I" in emails. Active voice where possible.
- Short sentences, one claim each. Give the size of an effect as well as
  its direction.

## Spelling and terms
- British English (-ise, colour, modelling).
- [Preferred term] rather than [term to avoid].

## Words I do not use
- groundbreaking, cutting-edge, paradigm shift, delve

## Formats
- Abstracts: 150 to 250 words. Problem, approach, main result with
  numbers, implication.
- Lay summaries: explain every technical term in everyday words.
- Emails: under 150 words, with the request in the first line.

## Examples
See examples.md for paragraphs that show the voice.

If you made custom styles in Claude before, they are being moved into skills automatically, and you switch them on in the same place.

A consistent visual style

Claude draws charts, diagrams and figures with code, so a style written down once is applied exactly every time.

Claude can show charts, diagrams and interactive visuals directly in a chat, and with code execution turned on it can write and run Python to produce figure files, or create slide decks and documents. It does not produce photographs or illustrations. Everything it draws follows instructions, which is why a visual style works best as a skill that contains the style file itself.

Define the style once

Decide on a colour palette that stays readable for colour-blind readers, the fonts and sizes at final print size, line widths, figure widths for the journals you publish in, and the export formats. The skill folder then holds the rules and a Matplotlib style file that applies them:

figure-style/ SKILL.md paper.mplstyle
The two files of the figure-style skillSKILL.md and paper.mplstyle, 33 lines
SKILL.md
---
name: figure-style
description: Use whenever I ask for a figure, chart, diagram or slide graphic
  for my research.
---

- For Python figures, apply paper.mplstyle from this skill with
  plt.style.use() before plotting.
- Use the palette in this order: #0072B2, #D55E00, #009E73, #E69F00,
  #CC79A7. Use grey #999999 for reference or background data.
- Tell series apart by line style or marker as well as colour.
- Axis labels in sentence case with the unit in brackets, e.g. "Stress (MPa)".
- Single-column figures 85 mm wide, double-column 175 mm.
- No titles inside figures. The caption carries the message.
- Diagrams and slides use the same palette and font.
- Save every figure as PDF and as PNG at 300 dpi.
paper.mplstyle
font.family: sans-serif
font.sans-serif: Arial, Helvetica, DejaVu Sans
font.size: 8
axes.labelsize: 8
xtick.labelsize: 7
ytick.labelsize: 7
legend.fontsize: 7
legend.frameon: False
axes.linewidth: 0.6
axes.spines.top: False
axes.spines.right: False
lines.linewidth: 1.2
axes.prop_cycle: cycler('color', ['0072B2', 'D55E00', '009E73', 'E69F00', 'CC79A7'])
figure.figsize: 3.35, 2.5
savefig.dpi: 300
savefig.bbox: tight
pdf.fonttype: 42

The palette above is the Okabe-Ito set, widely used because it remains distinguishable with the common forms of colour blindness. A group can agree one figure style, so that every paper and talk from the group looks related. Where a journal sets its own figure rules, those come first.

To check a figure, ask: "Compare this figure with my figure style and list every difference." The same works for a slide or a diagram.

Put it to work

With the profile, the rules and the two style skills in place, everyday research tasks need far less explanation, and the results look and sound like your own work.

Reading the literature
Upload papers to a project and ask for a summary of each, with a quote for every key claim so you can check it. The Research tool on paid plans searches recent work and returns cited summaries.
Writing papers
Turn your notes into an outline, then draft section by section. To edit a draft: "Revise this section in my writing style and list every change you made."
Responding to reviewers
"Make a table with each reviewer comment, my response, and the change in the manuscript with its line numbers."
Talks and posters
Ask for figures, diagrams and slides that follow your figure style, so a talk built in an afternoon looks like the rest of your work.
Outreach
Lay summaries, blog posts and posts for the group's website in your voice, from the abstract of a published paper.

On Team and Enterprise plans, projects and skills can be shared with colleagues. Without them, keep the style skills and the group's style guide in a shared folder so each member can upload the same versions.

Risks and good practice

You stay responsible for everything Claude helps produce. The Committee on Publication Ethics (COPE) states that AI tools cannot be authors, and that authors who use them for writing, images or analysis should say how and which tool in the methods or a similar section. Follow the policy of each journal and funder.

Claude can produce references that look real but do not exist, so take references from your own reference manager and check every number and claim against its source. A figure is only as accurate as the data and code behind it, so read the code or check the plotted values against your data.

On Free, Pro and Max plans, chats may be used to train future models unless you turn off Help Improve Claude under Settings > Privacy. Team and Enterprise plans are not used for training by default. Keep collaborators' details in your profile to public professional information, and share unpublished work only at the level of detail a task needs.

What you end up with

The same request, before and after the set-up in this guide.

Before the set-up

Draft the abstract from these notes.

This groundbreaking study delves into the behavior of novel bonded joints, showcasing cutting-edge insights for the field.

Fluent and generic, with hype words and no result. You correct it again in every chat.

After the set-up

Draft the abstract from these notes.

Bonded joints lose stiffness under cyclic loading, yet the rate is poorly quantified. We tested 24 specimens at two load levels. Stiffness fell by 18% (95% CI 15 to 21%) after 100,000 cycles.

British spelling and the result with its numbers, without being asked.
